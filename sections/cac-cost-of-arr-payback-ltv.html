Study package · SaaS unit economics

CAC, Cost of ARR, Payback, and LTV : CAC

Four metrics, one company, and four different answers to “which segment should we fund?” Every number below is computed correctly from the same seven inputs. The point of the package is to show you exactly which piece of information each metric holds that the others cannot see, and where each one lies to you.

Part 0

There is only one object here

Before the metrics: they are not four ideas. They are four questions asked of a single thing.

You spend money now to acquire a stream of gross profit over time. That is the whole of SaaS unit economics. One curve: a hole dug at the start, filled in monthly. Every metric in this package is a measurement taken off that curve.

  1. What did I spend per account? → CAC. A reading of the spend, divided by a headcount.
  2. What did I spend per dollar of the stream’s annual rate? → Cost of ARR. The same spend, divided by dollars instead.
  3. When does the filled-in part equal the hole? → CAC Payback. The zero-crossing.
  4. How much is the whole stream worth against the hole? → LTV : CAC. The area under the entire curve.

If you hold the curve firmly, you will never again be confused by a tab labelled “CAC Payback (Dollar).” You will just ask: which reading is this, off which curve?

Part 1

The case: Company A

Company A sells workflow software through three motions. Last year it spent $8.90M on sales and marketing and won $11.37M of new ARR across 1,302 new logos.

Everything in the table below is observed: things a real finance team measures directly. Nothing here is a metric yet.

The verdict CAC gives: self-serve costs $2,550 a customer and enterprise costs $216,667. Enterprise is 85× more expensive. Defund it and pour everything into self-serve.

Hold onto that verdict. By the end of this package it will be the single worst decision available to Company A, and CAC, computed perfectly correctly, points straight at it.

What only this metric can do
  • It is the only metric denominated in accounts. When your constraint is headcount, when the question is how many reps you need to land 40 logos, no dollar metric can answer it.
  • Quota and comp design. Reps carry deals, not dollars of blended efficiency.
  • Channel comparison at equal ACV. Inside one segment, where deal size is roughly constant, CAC is a clean read.
  • It is nearly a fact. You spent money; you counted logos. Almost no assumptions.
What it is blind to
  • There is no revenue in it. A cost with no benefit attached cannot tell you whether the spend was good. $216,667 is a bargain for a $310,000 account and a catastrophe for a $3,000 one.
  • It treats a logo as a unit of account. It is not one. Company A’s logos range over 100× in size and CAC counts each as 1.
  • Blended CAC moves on mix, not on performance, demonstrated below.
  • Company A’s blended CAC is $6,836, which describes no customer the company has ever acquired.

The phantom crisis

A company managed on blended CAC would spend this quarter explaining a crisis that did not happen, and would “fix” it by cutting the segment that caused the improvement.

Metric 2

Cost of ARR: what one dollar of revenue costs

The fix for a denominator that counts non-comparable things is to stop counting things and count dollars.

Cost of ARR = S&M spend ÷ new ARR won   units: $ per $, dimensionless
Self-Serve SMB

0.70x

Verdict flip #1. Enterprise spends 0.70 to buy a dollar of ARR; self-serve spends 0.85. The segment CAC called 85× worse is in fact the most efficient motion in the company.

CAC did not merely lose precision here. It pointed the wrong way. That is the difference between a noisy metric and a misleading one, and it is why the fix is never “use CAC more carefully.” It is to change the denominator.

What only this metric can do
  • Dimensionless. A pure multiple compares across segments, deal sizes, business models, and companies. “0.80x” means the same thing at a $2M company and a $2B one.
  • Self-weighting. A big deal adds to numerator and denominator proportionally, so mix shifts cannot manufacture phantom movement.
  • No allocation judgment about which logo is which. All ARR is ARR.
  • Still nearly a fact: both terms are observed.
What it is blind to
  • Blind to gross margin. A dollar of ARR at 81% margin and one at 62% count identically. They are not worth the same, and Metric 3 will prove it.
  • Blind to time. You paid up front; the ARR arrives in monthly slices. A multiple cannot be compared to your runway.
  • Blind to retention. It prices a dollar of ARR without asking how long that dollar survives.
  • Scope conventions vary. New-logo ARR only, plus expansion (gross), or net of churn (net). All three are called “Cost of ARR.” Pick one and never mix them in the same comparison.

What Cost of ARR actually claims, and what it does not

Two things trip almost everyone here, and both are about units rather than economics.

1. It says nothing about “forever”

It is tempting to read 0.78x as “we paid 78 cents to earn a dollar of revenue every year from now on.” It makes no such claim. ARR is a run rate: a snapshot of what today’s subscription base annualizes to, not a promise that it persists. Whether that dollar survives eight months or eight years is invisible to this metric; that is exactly the blind spot listed above.

Say it this way instead: Cost of ARR is what it cost to raise the annualized revenue run-rate by $1. No duration claim, stated or implied.

2. There is no period mismatch

The common worry: “we are dividing one month of spend by an annual revenue figure. Isn’t that apples to oranges?” It is not, and the reason is worth internalising.

The denominator is not “a year of revenue.” It is the increase in the run rate that this period’s activity produced. The word annualized describes the unit of that quantity, not the stretch of time it was accumulated over.

You spend $2,000 on a course in March and it raises your salary by $8,000 a year. Cost per dollar of raise = 0.25x. Nobody objects that “$2,000 is March and $8,000 is annual,” because the $8,000 / year is the size of the raise, and March is when you bought it. Cost of ARR is that sentence, applied to a company.

Formally: the numerator is a flow over a period (dollars spent in January); the denominator is a change in a stock over the same period (the run rate rose by so much during January). Both describe January. Matched.

The proof: it is period-invariant

Because both terms scale with the length of the period, they cancel. Company A at a steady run rate, computed three ways:

Computed overS&M spendNew ARR wonCost of ARR
One month$741,667$947,5000.783x
One quarter$2,225,000$2,842,5000.783x
Full year$8,900,000$11,370,0000.783x

Identical, every time. That is what being a pure ratio buys you, and it is why a workbook computing this monthly and a model computing it annually are both legitimately reporting “Cost of ARR.” A real period error looks different. One month of spend against a full year of new ARR (a genuine 12× mistake), or comparing this month’s spend to bookings it could not possibly have caused yet (the lag problem, which is about timing, not units).

Metric 3

CAC Payback: when you are made whole

Now change the question. Not how efficient was that, but I am out $2.60M, so when do I get it back? Two corrections turn Cost of ARR into that answer.

  1. Revenue is not repayment. When a customer pays you a dollar you do not keep a dollar, because hosting, support, and infrastructure eat part of it. What actually fills the hole is gross profit. So divide by GM%.
  2. It arrives monthly. ARR is an annual rate; you collect roughly one twelfth each month. So multiply by 12.
Payback = Cost of ARR ÷ GM% × 12   units: months

That identity is not a coincidence or a shortcut. It is what falls out when you take Cost of ARR and ask it to account for margin and for time. Payback is Cost of ARR, rescaled.

Where the ×12 comes from

That ×12 is the single most common sticking point in this formula, because it reads as “make the payback twelve times longer,” which sounds like nonsense. It is doing no economic work at all. Build the number without it and watch where it appears.

Do it with no formula

Company A is out $8,900,000. How much comes back each month?

It won $11,370,000 of ARR, and ARR is per year. So per month it collects $11,370,000 ÷ 12 = $947,500.

$8,900,000 ÷ $947,500 = 9.39 months on revenue, before margin

Done. No ×12 anywhere. The only 12 that appeared was a ÷12, and it appeared for an obvious reason: to turn a yearly rate into a monthly one.

The ×12 is that same ÷12, relocated

It only shows up if you insist on writing this in terms of Cost of ARR. Watch it migrate across the fraction bar:

$8,900,000 ÷ ( $11,370,000 ÷ 12 )
= $8,900,000 × ( 12 ÷ $11,370,000 )   dividing by a fraction = multiplying by its flip
= ( $8,900,000 ÷ $11,370,000 ) × 12
= 0.783 × 12 = 9.39 months

It is not a second step. It is the ÷12 seen from the other side of the fraction bar. Dividing the monthly income by 12 and multiplying the answer by 12 are the same operation.

Why it feels backwards: “×12” sounds like it lengthens the payback. It does not. It shrinks the monthly income by 12, which of course lengthens the payback. Shrink a denominator by twelve and the quotient grows by twelve. Same fact, stated from opposite ends.

The unlock: Cost of ARR is already a duration

Elsewhere this package calls Cost of ARR dimensionless, which is true under one reading, taking ARR as a plain dollar amount so that dollars over dollars cancel. There is a second reading, and the payback formula uses it:

$8,900,000$11,370,000 per year=dollarsdollars / year=years

Treat ARR as what it actually is, a rate, dollars per year, and Cost of ARR comes out in years. Company A’s 0.783x literally means 0.78 years of that revenue stream to recover the spend.

So multiplying by 12 is not a SaaS move at all. It is the same thing as saying two hours is 120 minutes. You are restating one duration in a smaller unit.

The version with no SaaS in it

You are owed $1,200. It comes back at $600 per year.

  • In years: 1,200 ÷ 600 = 2 years
  • In months: that is $50 a month, so 1,200 ÷ 50 = 24 months

24 = 2 × 12, not because anything changed, but because two years is twenty-four months. Nobody finds that ×12 mysterious. It is the identical operation.

So the formula, read honestly

Payback (years)  = Cost of ARR ÷ GM%        0.783 ÷ 0.701 = 1.12 years
Payback (months) = Cost of ARR ÷ GM% × 12  1.12 × 12 = 13.4 months

The ÷ GM% is the only economically meaningful adjustment in that line: revenue is not repayment, gross profit is. The ×12 is cosmetic: it exists purely because the industry quotes payback in months. If the convention were years, no 12 would appear in the formula anywhere.

Enterprise beat mid-market on Cost of ARR by a clear 0.10x and still loses here, entirely because enterprise carries a 66% gross margin against mid-market’s 81%: implementation teams, dedicated CSMs, custom infrastructure and SLAs.

This is the cleanest demonstration in the package. Neither CAC nor Cost of ARR contains the word “margin.” Both are structurally incapable of seeing what just changed the ranking. No amount of care in computing them would have surfaced it.

Payback throws away everything after the crossing point. A metric that stops reading at break-even cannot distinguish a customer who leaves the next month from one who pays for a decade. That is Metric 4’s job.

What only this metric can do
  • It is the only one that knows about gross margin. Two companies at an identical Cost of ARR with 85% and 45% margins are equally “efficient” and carry completely different cash risk. Payback exposes it; Cost of ARR hides it.
  • Denominated in time, so it is directly comparable to your runway, your sales cycle, your contract length, your churn horizon. A dimensionless multiple can be compared to none of those.
  • It is a working-capital statement. Growth consumes cash in proportion to payback. Short payback means growth funds itself; long payback means every new customer needs outside money.
  • Only one assumption (that margin holds). Still close to measurable.
What it is blind to
  • It assumes the customer is still there when the crossing arrives. The straight lines above imply a cohort that never churns. None of Company A’s do.
  • It ignores everything after break-even, which for a sticky segment is where nearly all the value lives.
  • Undiscounted by default. A dollar recovered in month 24 is worth less than a dollar spent in month 0.
  • It is silent on scale. A 10-month payback on $2M of spend and on $200M of spend are not the same business decision.

The correction

What payback cannot see: the cohort is shrinking

Every line in that chart is straight, which encodes an assumption nobody says out loud: 100% of the cohort is still paying in the month you break even.

Apply each segment’s actual gross retention to its own curve, discounting each month’s gross profit by the share of the cohort still alive to pay it, and break-even moves.

SegmentGross retentionStandard paybackCohort still payingTrue break-evenUnderstated by
Self-Serve SMB68%16.5 mo58.9%23.4 mo+42%
Mid-Market88%11.9 mo88.1%12.7 mo+7%
Enterprise94%12.7 mo93.7%13.1 mo+3%

Standard payback systematically flatters churny segments, and flatters them most, which is precisely backwards. Enterprise moves +3%. Mid-market moves +7%. Self-serve moves +42%, from a mediocre 16.5 months to 23.4 months, because by month 16.5 only 59% of that cohort is still paying.

Note what this does to the ranking: mid-market and enterprise were 0.9 months apart and are now 0.5 apart, effectively tied. The apparent gap between them was mostly an artifact of ignoring retention.

Metric 4

The only metric that reads past break-even. It asks what the entire relationship is worth, not when it turns positive.

Verdict flip #3. Enterprise takes it back at 5.25x, and self-serve is finally exposed: 1.66x, beneath every threshold anyone uses. The segment with the best CAC in the company is the one destroying value.

What only this metric can do
  • It is the only one that looks past break-even. Payback stops reading at the zero-crossing; for a segment retaining 94% a year, almost all the value is after that point.
  • It is the only one that prices duration. Retention enters no other metric in this package.
  • It puts cost and value in the same unit, so the comparison is direct.
  • It is the right frame for where to build: long-horizon segment and product bets.
What it is blind to
  • It is a forecast, not a measurement. Every other metric here is close to observed. This one requires you to predict churn years out.
  • 1 ÷ churn assumes a constant hazard rate. Real churn usually falls with tenure, so the formula is wrong in a direction that varies by segment.
  • It is most fragile exactly where it is most flattering. See the sensitivity grid below.
  • The famous “3x is healthy” benchmark is closer to folklore than evidence. Treat it as a smell test, never a gate.

Part 4

The question none of the four can answer

Every metric in this package is an average of what already happened. Company A’s actual decision is about the next dollar, and averages say nothing about it, because you exhaust your cheapest acquisition first, so the next dollar always costs more than the last.

The marginal figures below are stipulated for the example. They are the kind of thing you learn from channel-level tests, not from the P&L.

Where the next $1M goesAverage Cost of ARRMarginal Cost of ARRAverage paybackMarginal payback
Self-Serve SMB0.85x1.45x16.5 mo28.1 mo
Mid-Market0.80x0.72x11.9 mo10.7 mo

Self-serve’s paid channels are saturating: bidding against itself, rising CPCs. Enterprise’s next deals require standing up a new region: new leadership, localization, a 12-month ramp. Mid-market has an untested partner channel that tests better than its current blend.

The money should go to mid-market, which won exactly one of the four metrics, and lost the headline CAC comparison by 14×. Meanwhile self-serve, the CAC champion, has a marginal payback of 28 months and is the worst available use of the next dollar at Company A.

No reordering of the four metrics fixes this. It is a category the whole framework omits. When someone shows you a healthy blended payback, the question to ask is: what does the next dollar do?

The habit worth building: when someone quotes you one of these numbers, ask what it is blind to. CAC is blind to revenue. Cost of ARR is blind to margin, time, and retention. Payback is blind to retention and to everything after break-even. LTV : CAC is blind to nothing, and is therefore mostly assumption. There is no metric at the top of this ladder that is simply correct. Reliability falls as completeness rises.

Part 6

Check yourself

1. Blended CAC rose +88% year over year while every segment performed identically. What happened, and which number confirms it?

Mix shift. The company sold proportionally more enterprise, and enterprise logos carry a far higher CAC by construction because they are far larger. Confirm it with Cost of ARR (0.783x → 0.755x) or payback (13.4 → 12.9 months). Both improved. Better still, look at CAC by segment, where nothing moved at all.

2. Enterprise has the best Cost of ARR but loses on payback. Which single input causes that, and why can Cost of ARR never see it?

Gross margin: 66% for enterprise against 81% for mid-market. Cost of ARR is spend divided by revenue; gross margin appears nowhere in the formula. Payback divides by gross profit, so it is the first metric in the sequence where margin can matter.

3. Self-serve has by far the best CAC and the worst of everything else. What is the structural reason?

CAC has no revenue in it. It is a cost with no benefit attached. Self-serve buys logos cheaply ($2,550) but those logos are worth $3,000 a year against enterprise’s $310,000, a 103× difference CAC is structurally unable to represent. Cheap per logo, expensive per dollar.

4. Standard payback says self-serve breaks even at 16.5 months. Why is 23.4 the honest answer?

Because the straight-line curve assumes the whole cohort is still paying in month 16.5. At 68% gross retention only 59% of it is. Weight each month’s gross profit by the share of the cohort still alive and break-even slides to 23.4 months. The error scales with churn, so the flattery is largest exactly where the risk is.

5. Enterprise LTV : CAC is 5.25x at a 12% discount rate and 3.63x at 20%. What should you conclude?

That most of the number is the assumption, not the data. And note the asymmetry: self-serve barely moves across the same range (1.82x to 1.40x) because its value arrives soon. Long-lived segments carry their value far in the future, where the discount rate does the most work, so LTV : CAC is least trustworthy exactly where it is most flattering.

6. All four metrics describe last year. Name the question none of them can answer.

What the next dollar does. Every one of the four is an average over spend already committed; every real budget decision is marginal. At Company A the averages point at self-serve and mid-market, while the marginal figures say self-serve is the worst place to add a dollar (28-month marginal payback) and mid-market the best (11 months).

7. State the identity linking Cost of ARR to payback, and explain both adjustments from first principles.

Payback (months) = Cost of ARR ÷ GM% × 12.

÷ GM%: Cost of ARR is a revenue metric, but revenue does not repay you; only the gross-profit share of it does. × 12: Cost of ARR compares spend to an annual rate, and you collect roughly one twelfth of it per month, so the answer converts to months. Payback is not a separate formula. It is Cost of ARR asked to respect margin and time.

8. “0.78x means we paid 78 cents for a dollar of revenue every year from now on.” What is wrong with that sentence?

The words every year from now on. ARR is a run rate, a snapshot of what today’s book annualizes to, not a promise of persistence. Cost of ARR contains no retention term of any kind, so it cannot be making a claim about the future. The defensible sentence is: it cost 78 cents to raise the annualized run-rate by a dollar. How long that dollar lasts is Metric 4’s question, and it is the reason self-serve at 0.85x looks tolerable here and disastrous at 1.66x there.

9. Converting ARR to MRR divides by 12. Converting payback from years to months multiplies by 12. Why do the same twelves pull opposite ways?

They do not. It is one operation viewed from two sides of the fraction bar. Dividing by (ARR ÷ 12) is identical to multiplying by (12 ÷ ARR); shrink a denominator twelvefold and the quotient grows twelvefold.

The deeper answer: read ARR as the rate it is, dollars per year, and Cost of ARR is already a duration measured in years (0.78 years for Company A). The ×12 then does nothing but restate that duration in months, exactly as two hours is 120 minutes. The only step in the payback formula doing economic work is the ÷ GM%.

Methodology. Company A is a constructed case, engineered so the four metrics disagree; the inputs sit in ranges typical of real B2B SaaS. All figures computed from the seven inputs per segment in Part 1. No number is hand-entered.
